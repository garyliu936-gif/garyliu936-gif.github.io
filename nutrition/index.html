Foods That Are Toxic to Dogs — Never Feed These

Some human foods can cause serious illness or death in dogs. Every dog owner needs to know this list.

🍫

Chocolate

Contains theobromine — toxic to dogs. Dark chocolate and baking chocolate are most dangerous. Even small amounts can cause vomiting, seizures, and death.

Highly Dangerous
🍇

Grapes & Raisins

Can cause sudden kidney failure — even a small amount can be fatal. The exact toxin is unknown, so no "safe" dose exists. Avoid completely.

Highly Dangerous
🧅

Onions, Garlic & Chives

Damage red blood cells and can cause hemolytic anemia. All forms are dangerous — raw, cooked, powdered. Common in many human foods and sauces.

Highly Dangerous
🍬

Xylitol (Artificial Sweetener)

Found in sugar-free gum, peanut butter, candy, and some toothpaste. Causes rapid insulin release and liver failure. Check labels carefully.

Extremely Dangerous
🥑

Avocado

Contains persin, which can cause vomiting and diarrhea. The pit is also a choking hazard. Less dangerous than grapes, but avoid feeding it.

Moderate Risk
🍺

Alcohol

Even tiny amounts cause intoxication, vomiting, dangerous drops in blood sugar and temperature, and potentially coma. Keep drinks and food made with alcohol away from dogs.

Highly Dangerous
🌰

Macadamia Nuts

Can cause vomiting, fever, tremors, and weakness within 12 hours. Especially dangerous if combined with chocolate (common in cookies).

Moderate Risk
🍞

Raw Dough (Yeast)

Yeast continues to rise in the stomach, causing bloating and alcohol production from fermentation. Very dangerous — keep unbaked dough away from dogs.

Moderate Risk
If your dog ate something toxic: Call the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center: (888) 426-4435 or your vet immediately. Do not induce vomiting unless instructed.
How Much to Feed

Daily Feeding Guide by Dog Size

These are general guidelines. Always check your specific food's label and adjust for your dog's activity level and body condition.

Dog Size Weight Range Typical Breed Examples Daily Food Amount* Meals / Day
Toy Under 10 lbs Chihuahua, Yorkshire Terrier ¼ – ½ cup 3 (puppy) / 2 (adult)
Small 10–25 lbs Beagle, Shih Tzu, Pomeranian ½ – 1 cup 2–3
Medium 25–50 lbs Corgi, Border Collie, Bulldog 1 – 2 cups 2
Large 50–90 lbs Lab, Golden, German Shepherd 2 – 3 cups 2
Giant 90+ lbs Great Dane, Bernese, Cane Corso 3 – 5+ cups 2 (never 1)

*For a standard dry kibble at ~360 kcal/cup. Active dogs need more; sedentary or overweight dogs need less. Puppies need roughly 3× more calories per pound than adults.

Dry vs Wet vs Raw

Types of Dog Food — Compared

There's no single "best" food for every dog. Here's an honest comparison to help you decide.

🥣

Dry Kibble

Pros
  • Most affordable option
  • Easy to store and measure
  • Good for dental health (crunch helps)
  • Huge variety of formulations
Cons
  • Lower moisture content
  • Quality varies widely by brand
  • Highly processed
Best for: Most dogs, especially on a budget. Look for meat as the first ingredient.
Most Popular
🥩

Wet / Canned Food

Pros
  • High moisture — great for hydration
  • More palatable (picky eaters love it)
  • Often higher in protein
  • Good for senior or dental-sensitive dogs
Cons
  • More expensive per serving
  • Opens must be refrigerated and used quickly
  • Can contribute to tartar buildup
Best for: Senior dogs, picky eaters, and as a kibble topper for extra nutrition.
🥚

Raw Food (BARF Diet)

Pros
  • Closest to ancestral diet
  • Shiny coats reported by many owners
  • No fillers or artificial ingredients
Cons
  • Risk of bacterial contamination (Salmonella)
  • Time-consuming to prepare safely
  • Expensive and harder to balance nutritionally
  • Not recommended for immunocompromised dogs
Best for: Experienced owners who can follow strict food-safety protocols. Not for puppies or senior dogs.
🍳

Home-Cooked

Pros
  • Full control over ingredients
  • Great for allergy dogs
  • High palatability
Cons
  • Very easy to create nutritional imbalances
  • Time-intensive
  • Must be designed with a veterinary nutritionist
Best for: Dogs with complex allergies. Always consult a vet nutritionist to ensure balance.

Healthy Treats

Human Foods Dogs Can Eat

A quick reference for safe snacks — always in moderation, and never as a meal replacement.

🥕 Carrots Great for teeth, low calorie
🫐 Blueberries Antioxidant-rich superfood
🍎 Apple (no seeds) Vitamins A & C, fiber
🍌 Banana Potassium, magnesium — feed sparingly
🥜 Peanut Butter (xylitol-free) High protein, great training treat
🍗 Boiled Chicken Best for upset stomachs, pure protein
🥚 Cooked Eggs Protein, healthy fats, biotin
🍠 Sweet Potato (cooked) Fiber, vitamins — a favorite of most dogs
🥦 Broccoli (small amounts) Vitamins, but too much causes gas
🫘 Green Beans Low calorie, filling — great diet snack
🐟 Cooked Salmon Omega-3s for coat and brain health
🍉 Watermelon (no seeds/rind) Hydrating, vitamins A, B6, C
Nutrition Science

Feeding by the Numbers

10%

Treats should make up no more than 10% of your dog's daily calories to avoid nutritional imbalances and weight gain.

2×/day

Most adult dogs should eat twice a day — not once. One large meal increases bloat risk, especially in large breeds.

30%+

of a dog's daily calories should come from quality animal protein — look for named meats (chicken, salmon, beef) as the first ingredient.

7 days

is the recommended transition time when switching dog food brands or types — prevents digestive upset and loose stools.
