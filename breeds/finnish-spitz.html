Breed Overview

The Finnish Spitz (Suomenpystykorva in Finnish, meaning "Finnish pricked-ear dog") is Finland's national dog and one of the most distinctive hunting breeds in the world — a brilliant, fox-like Spitz that hunts birds using a hunting technique found in almost no other breed: the bark-point. Rather than scenting and pointing birds silently like a pointer or setter, the Finnish Spitz locates birds in trees, locks eyes on them, and begins a rapid, hypnotic bark — sometimes reaching 160 barks per minute — that both freezes the bird in confusion and guides the hunter to its location. This technique, called "bark-pointing" or "the Finnish method," has been refined over 3,000 years in the forests of Finland.

The Finnish Spitz's appearance is unmistakably fox-like: a dense golden-red coat ranging from pale honey to deep auburn, a sharply pointed muzzle, small upright ears, almond-shaped dark eyes, and a magnificently plumed tail curled over the back. Both males and females are strikingly beautiful, and the coat develops its full richness and density over several years — puppies are born dark and lighten dramatically as they mature. In Finland, the breed holds the honor of a "King of the Barkers" competition (Barking Champion) awarded to the top hunting dog of the season. In American homes they are vivacious, independent, and moderately trainable — retaining the Spitz spirit of doing things on their own terms.

Feeding Your Finnish Spitz

A small-to-medium, energetic hunting Spitz, the Finnish Spitz needs quality nutrition to fuel their active lifestyle. Their dense double coat benefits from omega fatty acid support.

Life StageDaily AmountCalories/Day
Puppy (under 12 mo)¾–1.5 cups350–700 kcal
Adult (1–9 yrs)1–1.75 cups450–750 kcal
Senior (9+ yrs)¾–1.25 cups350–600 kcal

Nutrition Tips

  • Small-to-medium breed formula supports their active hunting-dog metabolism
  • Omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids maintain the brilliant, dense double coat
  • Avoid overfeeding — the Finnish Spitz coat can mask weight gain; check body condition regularly
  • Puppies' coats darken and lighten dramatically as they mature — coat color nutrition is secondary to overall health

Fun Facts About Finnish Spitz

  • 🎵 Finnish Spitz can bark up to 160 times per minute — a rate so fast it sounds almost mechanical. In Finland, hunting competitions include a "King of the Barkers" (Barking Champion) title awarded to the dog with the best combination of hunting performance and ideal barking rate and quality. A dog that barks too slowly or too quickly is considered imperfect form; the ideal bark is rapid, clear, and rhythmic.
  • 🦊 Finnish Spitz puppies are born with dark, almost black coats that lighten dramatically over their first two years, ultimately maturing to the breed's signature golden-red. The coat transformation is so striking that owners who aren't expecting it sometimes worry their puppy is the wrong color — experienced breeders call this "going gold" and consider the gradual reveal one of the breed's most magical characteristics.
  • 🇫🇮 The Finnish Spitz is the national dog of Finland and is genuinely celebrated in Finnish culture — the breed appears on stamps, in folk songs, and in national art. In Finland, the breed is still actively used for hunting black grouse, capercaillie, and other birds in the vast boreal forests. There are more Finnish Spitz in Finland per capita than any other country, and Finnish hunters consider owning a top working Spitz a matter of national pride.
  • ⚠️ The breed came very close to extinction in the late 19th century, when urbanization and crossbreeding nearly eliminated the pure type from Finnish forests. In the 1880s, two Finnish sportsmen — Hugo Sandberg and Hugo Roos — mounted a deliberate conservation effort, traveling deep into rural Finland to find uncontaminated specimens and establish a true-breeding population. The modern Finnish Spitz owes its existence to their determined effort.
  • 🐦 The Finnish Spitz's bark-pointing hunting method requires remarkable self-control: the dog must freeze the bird's attention with rapid, mesmerizing barking while simultaneously wagging its tail slowly to signal to the hunter without alarming the quarry. This split-focus coordination — performing for two audiences at once — is considered one of the most cognitively complex hunting behaviors of any breed.
