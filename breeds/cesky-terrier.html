Breed Overview

The Cesky Terrier (pronounced "CHESS-kee") — also called the Bohemian Terrier — holds the distinction of being the only dog breed ever deliberately created in Czechoslovakia, and one of the very few breeds in the world whose origin can be traced to a specific person, a specific plan, and a specific date. In 1948, Czech geneticist and hunter František Horák set out to create a terrier better suited to hunting in the Bohemian forests — narrower-chested than existing terriers (to enter underground burrows more easily), calmer in temperament (to work better in packs), and easier to groom than wire-coated breeds. His solution was a carefully planned cross between a Scottish Terrier and a Sealyham Terrier, refined over years of selective breeding into a distinct and recognizable type.

The result is a low, long terrier with a surprisingly soft, silky coat — not the rough wire coat of most terriers — in shades of grey-blue or light coffee. Unlike most terriers, the Cesky Terrier is clipped rather than hand-stripped, and the characteristic "fall" of long hair on the legs, beard, and eyebrows gives them a refined, almost aristocratic appearance. Their temperament also differs from the typical terrier mold: the Cesky is calmer, less reactive, more tolerant of other dogs, and somewhat more trainable than most terrier breeds — all deliberate design decisions by Horák. AKC fully recognized in 2011, the Cesky remains one of the rarest AKC breeds.

Feeding Your Cesky Terrier

A small, moderate-energy terrier, the Cesky has modest nutritional needs. Their silky coat benefits from fatty acid supplementation, and their long-backed body needs weight kept in check.

Life StageDaily AmountCalories/Day
Puppy (under 12 mo)½–1 cup250–450 kcal
Adult (1–9 yrs)¾–1.5 cups350–550 kcal
Senior (9+ yrs)½–1 cup280–450 kcal

Nutrition Tips

  • Small-breed formula appropriate for their size and moderate activity level
  • Omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids maintain the silky, wavy coat
  • Keep lean — excess weight stresses the long back
  • High-quality protein supports their muscular terrier build

Fun Facts About Cesky Terriers

  • 🔬 The Cesky Terrier is one of the very few dog breeds whose creation is fully documented — František Horák's original breeding records, correspondence, and the precise dates and pairings of his Scottish Terrier × Sealyham Terrier crosses are preserved in Czech cynological archives. This makes the Cesky one of the most scientifically traceable breeds in history, with a paper trail from concept to recognized breed spanning less than two decades.
  • 🏅 The Cesky Terrier appeared on Czechoslovak postage stamps in 1965 — shortly after gaining international recognition — becoming one of the very few dog breeds to be commemorated on national postage during the lifetime of their creator. František Horák lived to see his breed recognized by the FCI in 1963 and celebrated nationally, a rare validation for a breed creator.
  • 😌 The Cesky Terrier was specifically bred to be calmer and less reactive than other terriers — and this temperamental difference is genuine and measurable. Where most terriers are described as high-strung, combative with other dogs, and difficult to train, the Cesky was designed (with specific Sealyham Terrier genetics) to be more biddable, pack-compatible, and manageable. Czech hunters needed a terrier that could work with other dogs in a pack without constant fighting.
  • ✂️ The Cesky Terrier's coat is unique among terriers in that it grows continuously like a human's hair and must be clipped regularly — it cannot be hand-stripped like most terrier coats. This was a deliberate design decision by Horák, who wanted a terrier that hunters could groom themselves without the specialized skill required for stripping wire coats. The distinctive "fall" of longer hair on the legs and face is left unclipped, creating the breed's characteristic aristocratic appearance.
  • 🌍 Despite being among the rarest AKC breeds in the United States, the Cesky Terrier enjoys considerably stronger numbers in Europe — particularly in the Czech Republic, Slovakia, Germany, and Scandinavia. Czech breed clubs maintain careful genetic diversity programs to manage the small worldwide population, and cooperation between European and American breeders is considered essential to the breed's long-term health.
