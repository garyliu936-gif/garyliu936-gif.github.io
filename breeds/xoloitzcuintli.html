Breed Overview

The Xoloitzcuintli (pronounced "show-low-eetz-QUEENT-lee") — often called the "Xolo" or "Mexican Hairless" — is one of the oldest and rarest dog breeds in the world, with a documented history spanning at least 3,500 years in Mesoamerica. Archaeological evidence of hairless dogs has been found at Aztec, Toltec, Colima, and Maya sites throughout Mexico and Central America, with clay figurines and burial artifacts depicting them as spiritual companions and guides to the underworld. The Aztec god Xolotl — deity of lightning and death — was said to have created the dog to guide human souls through the underworld, giving the breed its name: Xolo (Xolotl) + itzcuintli (dog in Nahuatl).

The Xoloitzcuintli comes in three sizes — Toy (10–14 lbs), Miniature (15–30 lbs), and Standard (30–55 lbs) — and in two coat varieties: hairless (the defining type) and coated (a short, dense coat). Hairless Xolos have smooth, tough skin that can range from dark charcoal and slate to liver, bronze, and spotted. Their body heat radiates warmly through bare skin — a trait that made them prized as living heating pads by Aztec people suffering from joint pain. They are calm, loyal, and deeply bonded to their family.

Health Notes

  • Skin care essential — sunburn, dryness, acne in hairless variety
  • Missing teeth common in hairless variety (linked to hairless gene)
  • Hip dysplasia (Standard size)
  • Generally a very long-lived, hardy breed (13–18 yrs)

Feeding Your Xoloitzcuintli

Feed according to size variety — Toy Xolos have very different caloric needs than Standard. All benefit from skin-supporting nutrition given their unique hairless coat.

Life StageDaily AmountCalories/Day
Puppy (under 12 mo)½–2 cups (by size)250–900 kcal
Adult (1–8 yrs)½–2 cups (by size)300–1,000 kcal
Senior (8+ yrs)⅓–1.5 cups (by size)250–750 kcal

Nutrition Tips

  • Omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids are critical for maintaining healthy, hydrated skin in the hairless variety
  • Vitamin E supports skin barrier — look for it in quality pet foods
  • Avoid overfeeding any size variety — their lean build makes excess weight very apparent
  • Dental health matters — missing teeth (linked to hairless gene) affect chewing; choose appropriate food texture

Fun Facts About Xoloitzcuintlis

  • 🏛️ The Xoloitzcuintli is one of the oldest dog breeds in the Americas — clay figurines of hairless dogs found at Colima burial sites in Mexico date to 300 BC, and DNA analysis suggests the hairless mutation in the breed's ancestors arrived in the Americas alongside the first human migrations from Asia over 15,000 years ago.
  • 🌡️ The Aztec people believed the Xolo's warm, bare skin had healing properties — the dogs were pressed against painful joints, the abdomen during illness, and aching limbs to transfer their body heat. While purely folk medicine, Xolos do radiate notably warm body heat through their bare skin, which modern owners still find comforting on cold nights.
  • 🎨 Frida Kahlo was famously devoted to Xoloitzcuintlis — she kept several at her home, Casa Azul, in Coyoacán, Mexico, and depicted them in at least two of her paintings. Kahlo and her husband Diego Rivera helped bring the breed back from near-extinction in Mexico in the 1950s by championing its recognition as a national heritage breed.
  • 🦷 The genetic mutation that causes hairlessness in the Xolo is linked to missing premolar teeth — most hairless Xolos are born with only partial dentition. This is not a defect but a genetic consequence of the hairless gene itself, and breed standards acknowledge and accept it.
  • 🎬 The Xoloitzcuintli gained significant pop-culture visibility in the 2017 Pixar film "Coco" — the spirit guide dog Dante is a Xolo, bringing worldwide attention to this ancient Mexican breed and sparking a notable increase in interest and registrations in the years following the film's release.
