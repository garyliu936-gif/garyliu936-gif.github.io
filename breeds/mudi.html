Breed Overview

The Mudi (pronounced "MOO-dee") is the rarest of Hungary's three herding breeds — the other two being the Puli and the Pumi — and the least known outside its homeland. A medium-sized, agile, curly-coated herding dog, the Mudi developed naturally on Hungarian farms in the 19th century, likely from crosses between spitz-type dogs and various herding and hunting dogs brought to Hungary by traders and settlers. It was formally recognized as a distinct breed by Hungarian cynologists in the 1930s. Unlike the Puli with its corded coat or the Pumi with its wavy terrier-like coat, the Mudi has a unique coat of tight, dense waves that is unlike any other breed in the world.

The Mudi is a true multi-purpose farm dog — it herds cattle, sheep, and pigs; hunts wild boar with courage that belies its modest size; and serves as a watchdog and companion. It is also one of the most naturally talented dog sport dogs, excelling in agility, flyball, frisbee, and obedience at a level that surprises those unfamiliar with the breed. The AKC recognized the Mudi in 2022. Outside Hungary, the breed remains very rare — finding a reputable breeder typically requires significant searching and patience, but those who find one discover an exceptionally capable, loyal, and entertaining companion.

Quick Facts

  • AKC Rank: Newly recognized (2022)
  • Group: Herding
  • Origin: Hungary
  • Also Known As: Hungarian Mudi, Canis Ovilis Fenyesi
  • Size: Small–Medium
  • Coat: Short on face/forelegs, medium wavy-curly elsewhere — unique texture unlike any other breed
  • Colors: Black, white, brown, yellow, grey, merle — wide color variety
  • Good for Apartments: No — a high-energy working breed needing substantial daily exercise

Health Notes

  • Hip dysplasia — OFA testing recommended for breeding stock
  • Epilepsy — seizures reported in the breed
  • Generally healthy with a small but dedicated breeding community maintaining health standards
  • Merle coloring — double-merle breeding must be avoided to prevent blindness and deafness

Fun Facts About Mudis

  • 🐗 The Mudi is one of very few herding breeds that also hunts — specifically wild boar, an animal capable of inflicting serious injury on a dog. Hungarian farmers prized the Mudi's fearlessness and agility for both herding livestock and assisting in boar hunts, making it one of the most genuinely versatile farm dogs in European history. This hunting courage is still evident in modern Mudis, which tend to be bold, self-confident, and undeterred by animals much larger than themselves.
  • 🌈 The Mudi comes in a wider range of colors than almost any other herding breed — black, white, brown, yellow, grey, blue merle, brown merle, and various combinations. This color diversity is unusual and reflects the breed's natural development without rigid early selection for color. In the show ring, all colors are equally acceptable, making the Mudi a visually diverse breed where no two dogs look identical.
  • 🏆 Despite being virtually unknown outside Hungary and very recently recognized by the AKC (2022), Mudis have been competing in international dog sport competitions for years and have demonstrated remarkable talent. Individual Mudis have won agility championships and international flyball titles competing against much more numerous Border Collies and Australian Shepherds — a testament to the breed's natural athleticism and the dedication of their handlers.
  • 🔬 The Mudi's distinctive coat — with short, smooth hair on the face and fronts of the legs, transitioning to medium-length, tight waves elsewhere — is unique in the dog world. No other breed has exactly this coat pattern and texture. It is self-maintaining to a greater degree than many coated breeds and does not cord like the Puli or require stripping like many terriers, making it one of the more practical of Hungary's herding breed coats.
  • 📉 The Mudi was nearly wiped out during World War II when Hungarian dog breeding was severely disrupted. Post-war reconstruction of the breed began with very few surviving specimens, and the modern Mudi carries the genetic legacy of that narrow bottleneck. Dedicated Hungarian breeders spent decades rebuilding the population, and today international interest — particularly following AKC recognition — offers hope for expanded genetic diversity through carefully managed breeding across borders.
