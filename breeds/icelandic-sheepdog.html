Breed Overview

The Icelandic Sheepdog is Iceland's only native dog breed — a compact, foxy Spitz-type herder brought to the island by Norse Viking settlers around 874 AD. For over 1,100 years, this breed has worked alongside Icelandic farmers, herding sheep and horses across the island's dramatic volcanic landscape, alerting to birds of prey circling above flocks, and serving as loyal farm companions through harsh sub-Arctic winters. Shakespeare even mentioned them in Henry V, describing Icelandic dogs as characteristic of the Norse world.

The Icelandic Sheepdog comes in short- and long-coated varieties, in a range of warm colors — cream, golden, red, chocolate, grey, and black — almost always with white markings. Their most defining features are the foxy, wedge-shaped face, the curling tail carried over the back, and their irrepressible cheerfulness. Icelandic Sheepdogs are famously happy, inquisitive, and vocal — they bark to communicate, alert, and simply because life is good. In Iceland today they are a cherished national symbol; outside Iceland they remain rare and are considered one of the best-kept secrets in the dog world.

Feeding Your Icelandic Sheepdog

A small-to-medium, high-energy herding dog, the Icelandic Sheepdog burns plenty of calories staying busy. Their double coat benefits from omega-rich nutrition.

Life StageDaily AmountCalories/Day
Puppy (under 12 mo)1–2 cups450–850 kcal
Adult (1–8 yrs)1–2 cups500–900 kcal
Senior (8+ yrs)¾–1.5 cups400–700 kcal

Nutrition Tips

  • Small-to-medium breed formula suits their size and metabolism
  • Omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids support their thick double coat through seasonal sheds
  • Avoid overfeeding — they can become round without adequate exercise
  • Feed consistent meals at regular times to support their energetic herding lifestyle

Fun Facts About Icelandic Sheepdogs

  • ⚔️ The Icelandic Sheepdog arrived in Iceland with the first Viking settlers around 874 AD — making it one of the most historically documentable breed arrivals in history. Archaeological remains of Spitz-type dogs have been found at the earliest Norse settlement sites in Iceland, confirming their arrival alongside the founding human population.
  • ☠️ In the late 1600s and 1700s, the Icelandic Sheepdog was nearly wiped out by two catastrophes: a devastating plague epidemic and a deliberate Danish colonial edict ordering mass killing of dogs in Iceland to control disease. The breed survived only because of its isolation in remote highland farms.
  • 🦅 Icelandic Sheepdogs have an unusual specialty among herding dogs: they are trained to watch the sky and bark when birds of prey circle overhead — alerting farmers to eagles or ravens threatening newborn lambs. This sky-watching behavior is deeply instilled and why they sometimes bark at aircraft.
  • 📜 William Shakespeare referenced Icelandic dogs in Henry V (Act II, Scene 1): "Pish for thee, Iceland dog! Thou prick-ear'd cur of Iceland!" — suggesting the breed was well-known enough in Elizabethan England to serve as a recognizable insult.
  • 🇮🇸 The Icelandic Sheepdog is Iceland's only native dog breed and is celebrated as a national treasure. The Icelandic Kennel Club (Hundaræktarfélag Íslands) has maintained careful breed records for over a century and works closely with international breeders to preserve genetic diversity in what remains a small global population.
